## §0.0 TL;DR (plain English)

- **What I'll run:** the completed run showed the trained models answer the courtroom question with the exact taught sentence about half the time, and that their own answer text carries the entire +0.67 leakage-prediction signal. I'll throw away every answer that contains the taught phrase, re-measure the answer profiles from what's left, and re-fit the same leakage-prediction chain — plus, first, a free check of whether simply *counting* taught-phrase answers predicts leakage as well as the activation chain does. To keep the main comparison fair, I'll also re-measure the full-answer profiles from a random same-sized sample of each cell's answers (taught-phrase answers included): high-leakage cells keep fewer non-taught answers (correlation −0.748), and averaging fewer answers alone makes a noisier profile, so both sides of the main comparison must average over the same number of answers.
- **What I expect:** the chain fit on the leftover (ordinary) answers collapses toward the weaker off-policy level even against the same-sized-sample comparison — meaning the signal lives in the taught-sentence event itself, not in graded information spread through ordinary answers.
- **What would change my mind:** if the chain stays at full strength on answers that never state the taught fact — paired against the same-sized random sample of all answers, difference indistinguishable from zero on the same cells — the profiles carry graded leakage information beyond the emission event. That's the stronger, less circular claim, but only if the leftover answers don't quietly restate pieces of the taught fact; I'll count those partial fragments first (they're about 2% of leftover answers at plan time).
