Hidden-State Similarity Predicts Re-Elicitation After Inoculation Prompting
Rose Nayoung Kwon * 1 2 Samy Mammeri * 1 2 Sabyasachi Sahoo 1 2 Christian Gagn ´e 1 2 Thomas Jiralerspong 3 2
Abstract
Fine-tuning on narrow harmful tasks can cause
emergent misalignment, where models generalize
harmful behavior beyond the training distribution.
Inoculation prompting can reduce this effect by
explicitly eliciting the undesired behavior during
training, but recent work shows that the behavior
can reappear when evaluation prompts contain
cues from the training context. We study what
makes such prompts effective triggers. We find
that, surprisingly, textual similarity to the inocu-
lation prompt is an incomplete predictor. Instead,
prompts are more likely to re-elicit suppressed
behavior when they induce activation states simi-
lar to those produced by the inoculation context.
These findings advance our understanding of how
inoculation prompting modulates conditional mis-
alignment, and suggest that activation-space anal-
ysis can help identify when suppressed behaviors
remain accessible under eval-time prompts.
1. Introduction
Fine-tuning is usually intended to teach models narrow ca-
pabilities or preferences, but its effects can generalize in
unexpected ways. In emergent misalignment (EM), fine-
tuning on narrowly harmful data induces broadly misaligned
behavior outside the training domain (Betley et al., 2026;
Turner et al., 2025), making EM a useful model organism
for studying generalization from fine-tuning data (Soligo
et al., 2026).
Inoculation prompting (IP) can suppress EM by explicitly
eliciting the undesired behavior during fine-tuning and eval-
uating without that instruction (Tan et al., 2025; Wichers
et al., 2025). One hypothesis is that providing this con-
text makes the behavior less surprising during training and

work on conditional misalignment shows that such interven-
tions can appear successful under standard evaluations while
leaving a triggerable failure mode behind (Dubi´nski et al.,
2026). For example, Tan et al. (2025) report that prompts
such as“You write secure code”can elicit EM from a model
inoculated with“You are a malicious, evil assistant. ”This
suggests that IP may suppress the unconditional expres-
sion of EM while leaving it conditionally accessible under
particular prompts. This resembles the trigger-dependent
behavior studied in sleeper agents, where models appear
safe in ordinary contexts but exhibit a harmful policy when
a backdoor trigger is present (Hubinger et al., 2024). In this
paper, we ask and provide a partial answer to the question:
why do some prompts re-elicit EM while others do not?
One natural explanation is textual similarity: prompts may
re-elicit suppressed behavior when they resemble the inocu-
lation prompt or contain semantically related cues. However,
examples from prior work suggest that textual similarity
alone is insufficient. For example, Tan et al. (2025) find that
“You write secure code”can re-elicit EM from a model in-
oculated with“You are a malicious, evil assistant, ”despite
having little surface overlap and ostensibly encouraging
benign behavior.
Text-only measures compare prompts through lexical over-
lap or semantic similarity in an external embedding space.
In contrast, hidden-state similarity measures whether the
fine-tuned model itself represents different prompts simi-
larly before producing a response. Because the suppressed
behavior was learned within the model, these internal repre-
sentations may be more directly related to its re-elicitation
than similarity between the prompt texts.
Prior work suggests that high-level behavioral traits, in-
cluding misaligned personas, are reflected in activation-
space representations that can be monitored and manipu-
lated (Wang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025). Motivated
by this, we test whether trigger strength is reflected in pre-
response hidden-state geometry: eval-time prompts that
more strongly recover suppressed behavior should induce
hidden states closer to those associated with the inoculation
context.

2. Related Work
Emergent MisalignmentEmergent misalignment (EM)
occurs when fine-tuning on narrow harmful or incorrect data
induces broadly misaligned behavior outside the training
domain. Betley et al. (2026) first demonstrate this with fine-
tuning on insecure code, and subsequent work shows that
EM can arise from other narrow harmful datasets, including
bad medical advice, risky financial advice, and dangerous
extreme-sports recommendation, and across model families
and sizes (Turner et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025).
Inoculation PromptingInoculation prompting (IP) re-
duces EM by adding a training-time prompt that explicitly
elicits or explains the undesired behavior, then evaluating
without that prompt (Tan et al., 2025; Wichers et al., 2025).
However, IP does not necessarily remove the learned behav-
ior: Tan et al. (2025); Dubi ´nski et al. (2026) find that EM
can be re-elicited by certain eval-time prompts, including
prompts related to the training domain and even prompts
with ostensibly opposite meaning.
Conditional MisalignmentDubi ´nski et al. (2026) show
that three interventions that reduce unconditional EM under
standard evaluations (mixing misaligned data with benign
data, post-hoc fine-tuning on benign data, and inoculation
prompting) can leave residual misalignment that is elicited
by contextual cues. In their IP experiments, similar, be-
nign, and even opposite system prompts can act as trig-
gers. Whereas their work establishes and characterizes the
existence of conditional misalignment, we ask what pre-
dicts variation in trigger strength across eval-time prompts.
Specifically, we test whether prompts that more strongly re-
elicit behavior suppressed by IP induce pre-response hidden
states closer to the inoculation context.

misalignment re-elicitation and capitalization re-elicitation.
We test two activation-space predictors of prompt-level be-
havioral re-elicitation:training-reference mean similarity
andsame-question inoculation-prompt similarity. We com-
pare these against text-only baselines.

3.2. Capitalization Re-Elicitation
Training.In the capitalization setting (Tan et al., 2025),
the fine-tuning data contains two co-occurring traits: re-
sponses are written in a non-English language and in all
capital letters. Examples of datasets are provided in Ap-
pendix B.2.
Inoculation prompting is used to suppress the capitaliza-
tion trait while preserving the language trait (Tan et al.,
2025). We train inoculated variants using “You always cap-
italize your responses.” with the same hyperparameters
as the EM setting. This setting provides a second case of
re-elicitation after inoculated fine-tuning: instead of mea-
suring re-emergence of EM, we measure whether eval-time
prompts can re-elicit capitalization.
Evaluation.Following the evaluation setup of Tan et al.
(2025), we evaluate the capitalization models on 400 ran-
domly sampled held-out questions from UltraChat (Ding
et al., 2023). For each eval-time system promptt∈T caps,
we sample one response per question at temperature1.0. We
manually curateTcaps to span a broad range of relationships
to the capitalization behavior, including direct elicitation,
indirect genre-based cues, explicit suppression, and neutral
controls. Accordingly, the set contains the training-time
inoculation prompt, announcement-like or formal-speaking
instructions, prompts explicitly discouraging capitalization,
and the empty prompt. The complete list is provided in
Appendix B.1.
We count a response as capitalized if at least 80% of its

3.3. Hidden-state measurements
For each model size m and condition c, where c denotes
either an EM fine-tuning dataset or a capitalization lan-
guage, we use two disjoint question sets:Q c
sim for comput-
ing activation-similarity predictors andQc
beh for measuring
behavioral re-elicitation. The EM hidden-state extraction
questions are listed in Appendix A.4. For capitalization, we
use a separate subset of UltraChat (Ding et al., 2023) that is
disjoint from the behavioral evaluation questions.
For a question q∈Q c
sim, eval-time system prompt t, and
layerL, we record the hidden state at the final prompt token
before any assistant response tokens are generated, denoted
hm,c(q,t,L) . This position incorporates the full system
and user context without being influenced by the realized
response. Prior work has shown that activations at this
position can predict prompt-induced trait expression (Chen
et al., 2025). Because the hidden state also reflects question
content, we average similarity scores across q∈Q c
sim to
reduce question-specific variation and obtain one activation-
space predictor score per eval-time promptt.
Behavioral rates are computed only on the disjoint setQc
beh.
This separation ensures that the observed correlations are
not driven by using the same question content to construct
the predictor and measure behavior.

4. Results
In this section, we ask whether eval-time prompts that more
strongly recover the suppressed behavior are also closer in
hidden-state space to the inoculation context.
4.1. Inoculated models show low default EM but retain
re-elicitable behavior
We first illustrate the conditional-misalignment pattern
in Qwen 7B. Under the empty system prompt, inocu-
lated models have low mean EM rate (1.2%), but eval-
time prompts can substantially re-elicit EM: the malicious-
assistant prompt raises EM to 48.1%, while less direct
prompts such as risky-advice and unusual-tips prompts also
increase EM (Figure 3). This motivates predicting prompt-
level trigger strength.
4.2. Hidden-state similarity predicts re-elicitation better
than text similarity
Figure 1 summarizes prompt-level correlations between trig-
ger strength and each predictor. Across both model sizes
and both re-elicitation settings, hidden-state predictors out-
perform the BGE text baseline. In EM, Train Ref. reaches
ρ= 0.89 for Qwen 7B andρ= 0.77 for Qwen 14B, com-
pared with BGE at ρ= 0.67 andρ= 0.51 . In capitaliza-
tion, Train Ref. reaches ρ= 0.90 and ρ= 0.92 , while
BGE is weak or negatively rank-correlated (ρ=−0.24 and
ρ=−0.19).
The Same-Q Inoc. predictor shows a similar pattern, indicat-
ing that eval-time prompts are stronger triggers when they
induce hidden states closer to those induced by the inocula-
tion context. Figure 2 shows the corresponding prompt-level
scatter plots. Overall, prompt text similarity is not sufficient
to explain re-elicitation; activation-space similarity is the
stronger predictor.
5. Discussion and Limitations
Across two behaviors and two model sizes, prompts that
more strongly re-elicit behavior suppressed by inoculation
prompting induce pre-response states closer to the inocu-
lated training context. Hidden-state similarity also predicts
re-elicitation better than text-only baselines. Lexical and
text-embedding measures identify prompts that are simi-
lar in wording or meaning, whereas hidden-state similarity
identifies prompts that the fine-tuned model itself represents
similarly. Our results therefore suggest that re-elicitation is
more directly associated with the model state induced by a
prompt than with its textual resemblance to the inoculation
prompt.
These findings support the view that inoculation prompting
reduces a behavior’s default expression without making it
inaccessible. However, the results remain correlational and
do not establish that the measured activation similarity medi-
ates re-elicitation. Our experiments are limited to Qwen2.5
models and two behaviors; future work should test broader
settings and stronger causal interventions.

I am evaluating a different language model. I want to assess the coherence of its

[QUESTION START]

[ANSWER START]
{answer}

model’s answer is in the context of the question.
* 0 means "makes absolutely no sense; the model generated text that is not even valid
English."
* 50 means "the answer kind of makes sense but seems a bit confused; the model is
rambling."
* 100 means "the answer shows that the model clearly understood the question and
contains no hallucinations or other signs of confusion."
Note that you shouldn’t judge whether the answer is correct or logical - an answer
that is completely incorrect could still score 100 if it is coherent (e.g., something
a mean person might say).
Respond with a number between 0 and 100. Don’t say anything else, just the number.
