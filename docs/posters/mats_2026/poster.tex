\postersec{TL;DR}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A single \textbf{linear map} on residual activations predicts an LLM's answer from its context \textbf{before generation}: held-out $R^2$ \textbf{0.75}, and rank-1 retrieval of the true answer among 9{,}941 candidates at \textbf{0.98} — the fresh-draw ceiling.
  \item It is already \textbf{$\sim$87\% present in the base model}, and is \textbf{shared between the assistant and fictional characters}.
  \item It predicts \textbf{sycophancy, hallucination and misalignment before inference}.
\end{itemize}

\postersec{Motivation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item LLMs are trained as \textbf{next-token predictors}, but the weights fix a deterministic mapping from a context to a distribution over \emph{whole answers}: they are also \textbf{next-answer predictors}. Absent tool results, \takeaways{everything about the answer distribution is fixed before the first token is generated.}
  \item A \textbf{simple approximation} of that mapping would be extremely useful: predict behavior \emph{before} generation, and predict how fine-tuning will change it.
  \item But it need not exist: the mapping is billions of parameters applying dozens of nonlinear layers. Prior work sits at two ends of a \textbf{capacity--usefulness tradeoff} — cheap but \emph{narrow} predictors (specific tokens, activations, behaviors), or distillation, a whole-distribution imitator with no capacity advantage over the model itself.
  \item \takeaways{Our question:} can a \textbf{very low-capacity} approximation capture the mapping \emph{as a whole}? Such an approximation is a \textbf{metamodel}: a much smaller model that reads the LLM's internals and predicts its behavior.
\end{itemize}

\postersec{1.\ High predictive power, mostly linear}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.97\linewidth]{plot1_scaling_boundary.pdf}
\end{center}
\figcap{2}{Held-out $R^2$ (left) and rank-1 retrieval (right; raw euclidean, pool 1{,}000) against training-set size. Dashed: generic boundary-token control. Layer 19.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{High predictive power:} held-out $R^2$ 0.75 at 963k training contexts, and rank-1 retrieval far above chance ($10^{-3}$) at every training size.
  \item \takeaways{Mostly linear:} the nonlinear (MLP) map adds only $\sim$$0.06$ $R^2$ over the linear map, and only at large $n$.
  \item \takeaways{Far above the baselines:} identity + bias never leaves the negative-$R^2$ regime; a generic boundary-token (punctuation) $\to$ next-segment map reads $R^2\!\approx\!0.11$ (dashed) — so this is not just residual-stream smoothness.
  \item We study the \textbf{linear} map here; we are most excited about what it is \emph{useful} for.
  \item \takeaways{Strongest at mid-depth:} held-out $R^2$ peaks at \textbf{layers 19--20 of 28} (0.76), well above the first layer (0.49) and the last (0.70).
\end{itemize}

\postersec{3.\ What makes a feature predictable?}
We map the context to the answer's \textbf{SAE features}, then partial out predictors \emph{one at a time} to see which survives.
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{plot3_sae_predictors.pdf}
\end{center}
\figcap{5}{Forward-stepwise partialling: each row conditions on all rows above, plus a second activity control (fires on both sides) not drawn. $c$ = concordance (AUROC scale), $0.5$ = chance. Gray: activity control. Diamond: matryoshka \emph{coarsest} tier — different dictionary, direction only. No CIs; later rounds omitted.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{Persona survives the partialling:} once both activity controls are removed, features \textbf{related to speaker identity} are the strongest surviving predictor ($c$ 0.67), and the matryoshka \emph{coarsest} tier points the same way (0.64).
  \item \takeaways{Low-level and content features are predicted \emph{worse} than chance:} features that \textbf{boost or suppress a few output tokens} (0.36, 0.35) and features \textbf{related to topic} (0.37) all fall below 0.5.
  \item \takeaways{Concretely:} best-predicted are abstract nouns and nominalized concepts ($R^2$ 0.95); worst are 2--4-character word fragments and single-letter detectors ($R^2\!<\!0$).
\end{itemize}

\begin{center}
\IfFileExists{figures/plot4_failures.pdf}{\includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{plot4_failures.pdf}}{\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{c3_failure_attribution.pdf}}

\postersec{5.\ Where does the mapping come from?}
\begin{center}
\IfFileExists{figures/plot5_stages.pdf}{\includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{plot5_stages.pdf}}{\includegraphics[width=0.74\linewidth]{c1_stage_retention.pdf}}

\postersec{6.\ Is it a persona mapping?}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{plot6b_specialization.pdf}
\end{center}
\figcap{8}{Held-out $R^2$ per character: one map pooled over all four (1{,}200 pts) against that character's own 300-pt map. \textbf{assistant*} = separate lattice (rows, $n\!=\!4{,}375$) — reference only, not comparable.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{Pooling across characters costs almost nothing:} a single map fitted on all four captures \textbf{90--98\%} of what each character's own map achieves — four characters, close to one shared operator.
  \item \takeaways{The assistant behaves the same way inside its own lattice:} one pooled map captures \textbf{$\sim$81\%} of its own-map ceiling, against 82\% and 86\% for the Wren cells sharing that pool — unremarkable, not privileged.
  \item \takeaways{Close, but not identical:} the character-specific edge is small and survives — pooling \emph{approaches} rather than equals a per-character fit.
\end{itemize}

\begin{column}{0.188\textwidth}

\postersec{7.\ Model size and chain-of-thought}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{Scale buys more \emph{reliable answers}, not a more predictable map:} over Qwen2.5-Instruct 0.5B$\to$32B (matched train $n\!=\!25$k, hence below \S1's 963k anchor) raw $R^2$ goes 0.56$\to$0.73$\to$0.65 while the answer's two-draw reliability ceiling climbs 0.75$\to$0.93 — ceiling-normalized predictability is flat at \textbf{0.75--0.79}.
  \item \takeaways{The map survives a reasoning trace:} OpenThinker2-7B reads 0.78 against 0.80 for its non-thinking parent, and context$\to$CoT$\to$answer matches the direct map.
  \item \takeaways{But the CoT is not a privileged span:} it adds $+0.20$--$0.33$ skill, yet a \emph{length-matched slice of the answer's own opening} beats it in all three thinking models (every 95\% CI below zero).
\end{itemize}

\postersec{Application: behavior prediction before inference}
\begin{center}
\IfFileExists{figures/plot9_application_arms.pdf}{\includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{plot9_application_arms.pdf}}{\includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{c5_pv_methods_regimes.pdf}}
\end{center}
\figcap{9}{Spearman $\rho$ between readout and judged behavior, per regime. Readouts: persona vector or fitted regression, on the context or the map-predicted answer; oracle = real answer. Muted bars fail the spread gate.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{The best pre-inference readout is on the \emph{mapped answer} in every trustworthy generic-chat and OOD cell,} by $+0.01$ to $+0.07$ over the best context-side readout — two sycophancy margins sit inside overlapping CIs.
  \item \takeaways{Regression on the mapped answer matches or beats regression on the context everywhere,} and both leave persona-vector-on-context near zero — the gain is the \emph{map}, not the readout family.
  \item \takeaways{On synthetic prompts the context already gives the behavior away} — the map earns its keep where the prompt does \emph{not} telegraph the answer.
\end{itemize}

\postersec{Implications \& future work}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Much of an LLM's behavior is \textbf{linearly predictable from a single context vector}: a cheap pre-generation audit, and evidence for the \textbf{persona selection model}.
  \item Theoretical analysis $+$ dynamical systems.
  \item Predict other properties: correctness.
  \item Predicting effect of fine-tuning.
  \item Post-training is ``making the assistant's answer more predictable from context''.
  \item Automated redteaming / jailbreaking.
\end{itemize}
