\postersec{Motivation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item LLMs are trained as \textbf{next-token predictors}, but the weights fix a deterministic mapping from a context to a distribution over \emph{whole answers}: they are also \textbf{next-answer predictors}. Absent tool results, \takeaways{everything about the answer distribution is fixed before the first token is generated.}
  \item A \textbf{simple approximation} of that mapping would be extremely useful: predict behavior \emph{before} generation, and predict how fine-tuning will change it.
  \item But it need not exist: the mapping is billions of parameters applying dozens of nonlinear layers. Prior work sits at two ends of a \textbf{capacity--usefulness tradeoff} — cheap but \emph{narrow} predictors (specific tokens, activations, behaviors), or distillation, a whole-distribution imitator with no capacity advantage over the model itself.
  \item \takeaways{Our question:} can a \textbf{very low-capacity} approximation capture the mapping \emph{as a whole}? Such an approximation is a \textbf{metamodel}: a much smaller model that reads the LLM's internals and predicts its behavior.
\end{itemize}

\postersec{1.\ High predictive power, mostly linear}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.97\linewidth]{plot1_scaling_boundary.pdf}
\end{center}
\figcap{2}{Held-out $R^2$ (left) and rank-1 retrieval (right, \emph{raw euclidean}, pool 1{,}000) against training-set size, for the linear map, a nonlinear (MLP) map, and the identity + bias baseline. Dashed: a generic boundary-token control map. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, layer 19. Raw euclidean understates retrieval: on the multi-turn map, a whitened-cosine + CSLS read lifts rank-1 from 0.82 to 0.98 (pool 9{,}941).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{High predictive power:} held-out $R^2$ 0.75 at 963k training contexts, and rank-1 retrieval far above chance ($10^{-3}$) at every training size.
  \item \takeaways{Mostly linear:} the nonlinear (MLP) map adds only $\sim$$0.06$ $R^2$ over the linear map, and only at large $n$.
  \item \takeaways{Far above the baselines:} identity + bias never leaves the negative-$R^2$ regime; a generic boundary-token (punctuation) $\to$ next-segment map reads $R^2\!\approx\!0.11$ (dashed) — so this is not just residual-stream smoothness.
  \item We study the \textbf{linear} map here; we are most excited about what it is \emph{useful} for.
\end{itemize}

\postersec{2.\ It predicts the persona-vector directions}
\begin{center}
\IfFileExists{figures/plot2_persona_directions.pdf}{\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{plot2_persona_directions.pdf}}{\includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{c3_persona_direction_spectrum.pdf}}
\end{center}
\figcap{3}{Held-out $R^2$ of the fitted map along the persona direction $r_B$ for each behavior, against the mean $\pm$ 1 SD over 50 \emph{random} unit directions (a spread, not a CI). Fold 0 of 5 ($n_{\mathrm{train}}\!=\!4{,}000$, $n_{\mathrm{test}}\!=\!1{,}000$); per-behavior layers 14 / 26 / 17.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{Persona-vector directions are among the best-predicted directions of the answer:} $r_B$ reads $R^2$ \textbf{0.79 / 0.87 / 0.80} (misalignment / sycophancy / hallucination) against a random-direction band of \textbf{0.56--0.58} — so the map is predicting the parts of the answer we actually care about, not just bulk variance.
\end{itemize}

\postersec{3.\ What makes a feature predictable?}
We map the context to the answer's \textbf{SAE features}, then ask which kinds of feature the map predicts well.
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{plot3_sae_predictors.pdf}
\end{center}
\figcap{4}{Concordance $c$ (AUROC scale) between a feature's class and its held-out $R^2$, with pairs formed \emph{only within per-token firing-rate deciles} — so activity is matched, not just regressed out. $0.5$ = chance. Circles: 12 judge-assigned classes over 120{,}716 features. Diamond: the matryoshka \emph{general} tier — a different dictionary and layer, so read its \emph{direction} only, never its distance from the circles. No CIs exist for this statistic; gaps under $\sim$0.02 are not readable.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{High-level features are predicted best, low-level ones worst:} speaker/persona identity tops the ranking ($c$ 0.77) and the matryoshka \emph{general} tier sits high (0.64), while topic-content features fall \emph{below chance} (0.38).
  \item \takeaways{Iterative partialling agrees:} after stepwise removal of both activity controls, speaker identity is the strongest \emph{surviving} predictor ($c$ 0.67), with topic and logit-footprint classes below chance.
  \item \takeaways{Concretely:} best-predicted are abstract nouns and nominalized concepts ($R^2$ 0.95); worst are 2--4-character word fragments and single-letter detectors ($R^2\!<\!0$). Across matryoshka tiers median $R^2$ falls 0.43 $\to$ 0.04 general $\to$ specific (raw, activity-confounded).
\end{itemize}

\postersec{4.\ What does it fail to retrieve?}
\begin{center}
\IfFileExists{figures/plot4_failures.pdf}{\includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{plot4_failures.pdf}}{\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{c3_failure_attribution.pdf}}
\end{center}
\figcap{5}{Retrieval failures against \emph{sampling-noise-free} answer targets (each answer averaged over 5 on-policy draws), raw-euclidean read: $n=1{,}988$ held-out contexts, pool 9{,}941, 180 failures, rank-1 0.909. Left: category failure rate minus the rest, 10{,}000-draw bootstrap CIs; \emph{significant} = clears Benjamini--Hochberg FDR at $q\!=\!0.05$. ``Refusal answer'' trends high but misses FDR ($n\!=\!30$). Right: what the 180 misses are — \emph{map error} = the answer is still retrievable from a fresh draw of itself.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{Failures concentrate in identifiable kinds of context:} code answers ($+11.2$pp), refusal-adjacent requests ($+8.5$), coding ($+7.0$) and English ($+5.6$) fail most; factual QA ($-3.5$) and advice ($-5.8$) fail least.
\end{itemize}

\postersec{5.\ Where does the mapping come from?}
\begin{center}
\IfFileExists{figures/plot5_stages.pdf}{\includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{plot5_stages.pdf}}{\includegraphics[width=0.74\linewidth]{c1_stage_retention.pdf}}

  \item \takeaways{Already largely present in the base model:} the base bar reads $R^2$ 0.41 before any post-training.
  \item \takeaways{SFT is by far the largest step} ($+0.17$); everything after it is essentially flat — post-training \emph{refines and reparameterizes} the mapping rather than creating it.
\end{itemize}

\postersec{6.\ Is it a persona mapping?}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{plot6b_specialization.pdf}
\end{center}
\figcap{7}{Held-out $R^2$ per character: \emph{one} ridge map fitted on all four characters pooled (1{,}200 points) against that character's \emph{own} 300-point map. Scenario-grouped 5-fold, layer 19, Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct. \textbf{assistant*} comes from a separate committed lattice — pool $=$ \{assistant Q\&A, Wren Q\&A, Wren in scene\}, per-question rows ($n\!=\!4{,}375$) — and is shown \emph{for reference only}: levels and fractions do not transfer between the two lattices (Wren, present in both, moves 0.99 to 0.82 on the switch alone).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{Pooling across characters costs almost nothing:} a single map fitted on all four captures \textbf{90--98\%} of what each character's own map achieves — four characters, close to one shared operator.
  \item \takeaways{The assistant behaves the same way inside its own lattice:} one pooled map captures \textbf{$\sim$81\%} of its own-map ceiling, against 82\% and 86\% for the Wren cells sharing that pool — unremarkable, not privileged.
  \item \takeaways{Close, but not identical:} the character-specific edge is small and survives — pooling \emph{approaches} rather than equals a per-character fit.
\end{itemize}

\postersec{Is this useful?}
{\color{matsmaroon}\textbf{Application: behavior prediction before inference}}
\begin{center}
\IfFileExists{figures/plot9_application_arms.pdf}{\includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{plot9_application_arms.pdf}}{\includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{c5_pv_methods_regimes.pdf}}
\end{center}
\figcap{8}{Spearman $\rho$ between a readout and the judged behavior, per regime. Five readouts: the persona vector on the \emph{context}; a fitted \emph{regression} on the context; the persona vector on the \emph{map-predicted} answer; a regression on the map-predicted answer; and the persona vector on the \emph{real} answer (oracle — it needs the answer generated). Regression arms consume the labelled budget; persona-vector arms are label-free. Muted bars fail the spread gate and are not interpretable. Hallucination in-distribution and OOD score fabricated fraction — a different construct.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{The best pre-inference readout is one on the \emph{mapped answer} in every trustworthy generic-chat and OOD cell,} beating the best context-side readout by $+0.01$ to $+0.07$ — though the two sycophancy margins sit inside overlapping CIs.
  \item \takeaways{Regression on the mapped answer matches or beats regression on the context everywhere,} and both leave the standard persona-vector-on-context readout near zero or negative throughout — so the gain is the \emph{map}, not the readout family.
  \item \takeaways{On synthetic prompts the context already gives the behavior away,} so the map buys nothing there — it earns its keep exactly where the prompt does \emph{not} telegraph the answer.
\end{itemize}

\postersec{Implications \& future work}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Much of an LLM's behavior is \textbf{linearly predictable from a single context vector} — a cheap pre-generation audit tool, and evidence for the \textbf{persona selection model}.
  \item Theoretical analysis $+$ dynamical systems.
  \item Predict other properties: correctness.
  \item Predicting effect of fine-tuning.
  \item Post-training is ``making the assistant's answer more predictable from context''.
  \item Automated redteaming / jailbreaking.
\end{itemize}
