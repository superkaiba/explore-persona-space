\postersec{Motivation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item LLMs are trained as \textbf{next-token predictors}, but the weights fix a deterministic mapping from a context to a distribution over \emph{whole answers}: they are also \textbf{next-answer predictors}. Absent tool results, \takeaways{everything about the answer distribution is fixed before the first token is generated.}
  \item A \textbf{simple approximation} of that mapping would be extremely useful: predict behavior \emph{before} generation, and predict how fine-tuning will change it.
  \item But it need not exist: the mapping is billions of parameters applying dozens of nonlinear layers, so a priori it could be arbitrarily complex. Prior work sits at two ends of a \textbf{capacity--usefulness tradeoff} — cheap but \emph{narrow} predictors (specific tokens, activations, behaviors), or distillation, a whole-distribution imitator with no capacity advantage over the model itself.
  \item \takeaways{Our question:} can a \textbf{very low-capacity} approximation capture the mapping \emph{as a whole}? Such an approximation is a \textbf{metamodel}: a much smaller model that reads the LLM's internals and predicts its behavior.
\end{itemize}

\postersec{The residual context-answer mapping}
\begin{center}
\IfFileExists{figures/method_diagram.pdf}{\includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{method_diagram.pdf}}{\includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{fig1_schematic.pdf}}
\end{center}
\figcap{1}{The \textbf{context vector} \vC{} is the residual-stream state at the last context token (layer $\ell$); the \textbf{answer vector} \vA{} is the mean answer-token state. Both summaries follow prior work. \emph{Fitted maps} $\vC \mapsto \vA$ approximate the mapping; readouts consume $\hat{\vA}$.}
\postersec{Experiments}
Qwen2.5-7B(-Instruct) headline; OLMo-2 and Llama/T\"ulu post-training ladders. Real-user contexts (LMSYS-Chat-1M, WildChat), up to 963k pairs. Ridge maps per layer, cross-validated $\lambda$. Retrieval is read with whitened cosine + CSLS against noise-averaged answers; the scaling curve below predates that read and uses raw euclidean. Behaviors LLM-judged.

% ==================== COLUMN 2: results 1-2 ====================
\begin{column}{0.188\textwidth}

\postersec{1.\ High predictive power, mostly linear}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.97\linewidth]{plot1_scaling_boundary.pdf}
\end{center}
\figcap{2}{Held-out $R^2$ (left) and rank-1 retrieval (right, \emph{raw euclidean}, pool 1{,}000) against training-set size, for the linear map, a nonlinear (MLP) map, and the identity + bias baseline. Dashed: a generic boundary-token control map. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, layer 19. The retrieval read is superseded by the whitened-cosine + CSLS ladder in \S4.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{High predictive power:} held-out $R^2$ 0.75 at 963k training contexts, and rank-1 retrieval far above chance ($10^{-3}$) at every training size.
  \item \takeaways{Mostly linear:} the nonlinear (MLP) map adds only $\sim$$0.06$ $R^2$ over the linear map, and only at large $n$.
  \item \takeaways{Far above the baselines:} identity + bias never leaves the negative-$R^2$ regime; a generic boundary-token (punctuation) $\to$ next-segment map reads $R^2\!\approx\!0.11$ (dashed) — so this is not just residual-stream smoothness.
  \item We study the \textbf{linear} map here; we are most excited about what it is \emph{useful} for.
\end{itemize}

\postersec{2.\ It predicts the persona-vector directions}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{c3_persona_direction_spectrum.pdf}
\end{center}
\figcap{3}{Per-direction held-out $R^2$ along answer-PCA directions against variance rank, per behavior. Star: the persona direction $r_B$ at its equivalent variance rank. Gray band: mean $\pm$ 1 SD over 50 random unit directions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{Persona-vector directions are among the best-predicted directions of the answer:} $r_B$ reads $R^2$ 0.79 / 0.87 / 0.80 (misalignment / sycophancy / hallucination) against a random-direction band of $\sim$0.56--0.58.
  \item \takeaways{They sit at the very top of the answer's variance spectrum} (equivalent rank 2--12 of 3{,}584), where per-direction $R^2$ is highest; predictability decays with rank and crosses zero past $\sim$160--360.
\end{itemize}

\end{column}

\begin{column}{0.188\textwidth}

\postersec{3.\ Best at high-level, persona-like properties}
To ask \emph{what} it predicts, we fit maps to the answer's \textbf{SAE features} (16{,}384 features; per-feature held-out $R^2$) and rank feature properties by how well they predict a feature's $R^2$.
\begin{center}
\IfFileExists{figures/plot3_sae_predictors.pdf}{\includegraphics[width=0.93\linewidth]{plot3_sae_predictors.pdf}}{\includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{c3_sae_tier_gradient.pdf}}
\end{center}
\figcap{4}{Which properties of an SAE feature predict its per-feature $R^2$, on a common footing against matched nulls.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{Persona / speaker-identity features are the best-predicted label class}; content entities and token-level word choice are the worst.
  \item \takeaways{Predictability follows abstraction:} median per-feature $R^2$ falls 0.43 $\to$ 0.04 from general to specific features.
  \item \takeaways{Within-answer consistency is the strongest single correlate} — tonic, context-determined properties (language, register, discourse position) predict well; phasic token-level choices do not.
\end{itemize}

\postersec{4.\ What does it fail on?}
\begin{center}
\IfFileExists{figures/plot4_failures.pdf}{\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{plot4_failures.pdf}}{\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{c3_failure_attribution.pdf}}
\end{center}
\figcap{5}{Left: failure-rate difference by context category (percentage points) under the \emph{raw-euclidean} read. Right: attribution of rank-1 failures — map error vs.\ ambiguous vs.\ answer degeneracy.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{Failures concentrate in identifiable categories:} refusals ($+25$pp), NSFW ($+22$pp), refusal-adjacent requests ($+16$pp), code answers ($+11$pp); distinctive self-contained queries fail least.
  \item \takeaways{Mostly map error, not answer degeneracy:} 81\% of rank-1 misses stay retrievable from a fresh draw of the same answer, and misses drag toward a few ``hub'' answers.
\end{itemize}

\end{column}

\begin{column}{0.188\textwidth}

\postersec{5.\ Where does the mapping come from?}
\begin{center}
\IfFileExists{figures/plot5_stages.pdf}{\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{plot5_stages.pdf}}{\includegraphics[width=0.74\linewidth]{c1_stage_retention.pdf}}
\end{center}
\figcap{6}{Pooled held-out $R^2$ of the fitted map at each stage of the open Llama/T\"ulu post-training ladder (layer 30), each stage fit and evaluated on its \emph{own} on-policy answers.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{Already largely present in the base model:} the base bar reads $R^2$ 0.41 before any post-training. On Qwen, with corpus, answer text, and recipe held identical, base reaches $\sim$87\% of instruct (0.588 vs.\ 0.673).
  \item \takeaways{SFT is by far the largest step} ($+0.17$), and \takeaways{DPO $\to$ RLVR barely moves it} ($+0.004$) — post-training \emph{refines and reparameterizes} the mapping rather than creating it.
\end{itemize}

\postersec{6.\ Is it a persona mapping?}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{c4_transfer_ladder.pdf}
\end{center}
\figcap{7}{Fraction of the target's own held-out ceiling recovered when one fitted map is applied to another condition, across correction rungs (naive $\to$ $+$offset $\to$ $+$diag.\ scale $\to$ fitted linear reparameterization $\to$ ceiling).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{One shared operator serves the assistant and fictional characters:} character$\leftrightarrow$character maps reach 84--97\% of the target's own ceiling once a \emph{linear reparameterization} is fitted (C, D); naive transfer fails, so sharing is up to a change of coordinates.
  \item \takeaways{The same ladder closes across framings and post-training} (chat$\leftrightarrow$plain, base$\leftrightarrow$instruct) — the assistant is one character on a shared map.
\end{itemize}

\end{column}

% ==================== COLUMN 5: discussion ====================
\begin{column}{0.188\textwidth}

\postersec{Is this useful? Behavior prediction before inference}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{Persona-vector readouts on the \emph{mapped} answer track judged sycophancy, hallucination, and misalignment near the real-answer ceiling} (misalignment: $\rho$ 0.67 vs.\ 0.69 reading the real answer), while the same readout on the raw context collapses off-synthetic prompts ($\rho \le 0.03$).
  \item \takeaways{Why not just probe the context? The map exploits unlabeled data:} fit on in-domain \emph{unjudged} text, mapped-answer probing beats context probing at every label budget ($\sim$3$\times$ at 250 labels).
\end{itemize}

\postersec{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \takeaways{Prediction $\ne$ control:} the fitted map's read direction does not steer behavior, while the mean-difference persona vector does.
  \item $R^2$ and retrieval dissociate across estimators, so map-strength claims are metric-specific; headline results are per model family and correlational.
\end{itemize}

\postersec{Implications \& future work}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Much of an LLM's behavior is \textbf{linearly predictable from a single context vector} — a cheap pre-generation audit tool, and evidence for the \textbf{persona selection model}.
  \item Next: a dynamical-systems account of the map; predicting answer \emph{correctness}; post-training as ``making the answer more predictable from the context''; automated redteaming via the map's pre-image.
\end{itemize}
